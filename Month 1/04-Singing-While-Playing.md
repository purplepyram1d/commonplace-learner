# 04 — Singing While Playing

This is the most important document in the course. Almost everyone can learn chords, and almost everyone can learn a melody. **Doing both at once is a separate skill**, and it's the one most beginners never practice deliberately, so they give up.

---

## 1. Why it's hard

Your brain has limited **conscious attention**. When you start:
- The **left hand** needs attention (which chord, where fingers go)
- The **right hand** needs attention (strumming pattern, timing)
- The **voice** needs attention (pitch, words, breathing)

That's three attention-hungry jobs, and you can consciously handle about one. The result is the classic beginner crash: you start singing and your strumming stops or stutters, or you keep strumming and the words vanish.

### The solution: automate the guitar
The goal is to make the guitar part **automatic** (handled by "muscle memory," the procedural motor system), so your conscious attention is free for singing. You know this from ordinary life: you can walk and talk at the same time because walking is automatic. **You need your strumming to become "walking."**

**The guitar must become automatic *before* you add the voice.** This is why Week 3 has you play the progression far more than seems necessary.

### The second problem: two different rhythms
Your **strumming** follows a steady grid (D - D U - U D U). Your **words** follow the melody's rhythm, which often lands **between** strums, starts **before** the chord change (pickups), or holds **across** chord changes. Your brain wants to "sync" them (making your hand follow your voice or vice versa). You have to train them to be **independent**.

---

## 2. The Layering Method (the core technique)

Add **one layer at a time**. Only move to the next step when the current one feels **boring**, meaning you can do it without thinking.

| Step | What you do | Success = | Week |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Guitar alone** | Play the progression/section with the target strum | 3 clean run-throughs in a row, no stopping | 2–3 |
| **2. Guitar + counting aloud** | Play while counting "1, 2, 3, 4" out loud | Counting never stops, strum never stops | 2 |
| **3. Guitar + talking** | Play while having a conversation, reciting the alphabet, or describing your day | You can talk about something unrelated without stopping | 2–3 |
| **4. Guitar + humming** | Play while humming the melody (**no words**) | Hum stays in tune, strum stays steady | 3 |
| **5. Guitar + speaking the lyrics in rhythm** | Say (don't sing) the words in the melody's rhythm while playing | Words land at the right time over the right chords | 3 |
| **6. Simplified guitar + singing** | Sing the real melody while strumming **once per chord** (whole notes) | Your voice leads; the guitar just "marks" the chord changes | 3 |
| **7. Simple strum + singing** | Sing while doing 4 downstrums per bar | Steady quarter-note downs under the melody | 3–4 |
| **8. Full pattern + singing** | Sing with the full strum pattern | The whole thing | 4 |

**If something falls apart, drop back one step.** This isn't failure. It's exactly how the method works. Professionals do this with every new song.

---

## 3. The micro-level: syllable-strum mapping

When a line won't come together, zoom in to **which syllable lands on which strum.**

### How to map a line
1. Play the line's chords **very slowly** (50–60 BPM) with **quarter-note downs** (4 per bar).
2. **Speak** the words slowly in rhythm.
3. Notice which syllables line up **exactly with a down-strum** and which fall **between** strums.
4. Write it out, e.g. (using `↓` for a downstrum on the beat):

```
Beat:        1       2       3       4     | 1 ...
Strum:       ↓       ↓       ↓       ↓     | ↓
Words:  (pickup)  ...syllable lands here... | next chord lands with this syllable
```

5. Mark the **anchor syllables**: the ones that land **on beat 1** of a bar or **on a chord change**. These are your "sync points." If you nail the anchors, the syllables in between take care of themselves.

The [Song Guide](05-Song-Guide-Aint-No-Sunshine.md) shows where chord changes fall in each line. Use the recording plus your lyric sheet to mark your own anchor syllables with a pencil. **This act of mapping is itself practice.** Do it.

### Pickup notes (singing before the chord)
Many lines in this song start **before beat 1**. The first word or two come in during the end of the previous bar, and then the new chord arrives. Beginners want to wait for the strum and sing on it, which makes them late.

**Fix:** count out loud "1, 2, 3, 4" through the previous bar, and start singing on "4" or "&" (whatever the recording does). Practice just the transition: **last bar of line → pickup → first chord of next line**, 10 times in a row.

## 4. Drills for independence

Do 3–5 minutes of these most days in Weeks 2–4.

### Drill A: Talk & strum
Strum Am with 4 downstrums per bar at 70 BPM. While strumming:
- Count to 20 out loud
- Say the alphabet
- Describe what you had for breakfast
- Answer questions someone asks you

**Why:** it trains your strumming to run on autopilot while your language brain does something else.

### Drill B: Off-beat claps and speech
Strum quarter notes (beats 1 2 3 4) and say "and" **between** each strum: "↓ and ↓ and ↓ and ↓ and." Then say a word only on the "&"s. **Why:** words don't always land on strums, and this trains the separation.

### Drill C: Long note, moving hand
Strum 8 downstrums (2 bars) of Am while singing **one long note** (hum an A or E). Then do Em while singing a long E or B. **Why:** holding a note while your hand keeps moving is exactly what happens at the ends of this song's lines.

### Drill D: Moving voice, still hand
Strum **once** (Am), let it ring for 4 beats, and sing a 5-note scale over it. **Why:** gets your voice comfortable moving over a steady chord without the hand interfering.

### Drill E: Change on the word
Strum Am. Say the word "change" on beat 1 of every other bar, **and switch to Em exactly on the word**, then back. **Why:** trains linking a word to a chord change. In the song, specific syllables happen right at the chord change.

### Drill F: The "stop and go"
Play the progression. Randomly (or when a friend claps) stop strumming but **keep singing**, then restart strumming **on the right beat**. **Why:** proves your voice isn't dependent on the guitar, and trains you to recover from mistakes.

### Drill G: Eyes up
Once a section feels comfortable, play it **looking at a spot on the wall**, not your hands. Singers perform to people, not to their fretboard. Also, looking down squashes your throat.

## 5. Rules for when you're singing and playing

1. **The strumming hand never stops.** If you miss a chord, keep strumming (open strings, muted strings, whatever) and catch the next change. **Stopping is the real mistake**, not a wrong chord.
2. **The voice leads.** If the two get out of sync, keep singing and let the guitar catch up at the next bar line. Listeners follow the voice.
3. **Guitar volume < voice volume.** Strum lighter than you think.
4. **Simplify under pressure.** Hard section? Drop to whole-note or quarter-note strums just for that part. Nobody will notice. Pro players simplify all the time while singing.
5. **Breathe where you planned.** Mark breaths on your lyric sheet.
6. **Posture.** Head up, chin level, chest open. Hunched over the guitar = choked voice.
7. **Always start with a count-in.** Say (or think) "1, 2, 3, 4," strum 2 or 4 bars of Am as an intro, then start singing. Never start the voice and guitar "cold" together.

---

## 6. Mental practice (it's real practice)

Research on motor learning consistently shows that **mentally rehearsing** a skill improves performance. Use dead time (commute, falling asleep, waiting in line):
- Picture your left hand forming Am → Em → G → Am, feeling each finger.
- "Hear" the song in your head and imagine your strumming hand moving.
- Mentally sing a verse and imagine the chord changes happening under specific words.

It costs your fingers and voice nothing. It's especially useful in Week 3, when your fingers are tired but your brain can keep working.

---

## 7. How you'll know it's working

| Stage | Sign |
|---|---|
| Beginning | Everything stops when you start singing |
| Early | You can do it, but only when you're thinking hard, and you're exhausted after one verse |
| Middle | You can get through it, but you can't tell me later whether your strumming was steady |
| **Target for Day 28** | You can sing the song and your hand "just goes." You can listen to your own voice while playing |
| Beyond | You can play and sing while smiling at someone, and adjust when they sing along |

It's normal to be at the "early" or "middle" stage for most of Weeks 3–4. The jump to "target" often happens suddenly, after a good night's sleep.
