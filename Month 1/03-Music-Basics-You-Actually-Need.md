# 03 — Music Basics You Actually Need

This is just enough theory to understand what you're doing in Month 1, and nothing more. You can play without knowing any of it, but knowing it makes everything make sense and speeds up learning songs later.

---

## 1. Notes: the musical alphabet

Music uses only **7 letter names**: **A B C D E F G**, then it starts over at A (one octave higher).

Between most letters there's an extra note, a **sharp (♯)** or **flat (♭)**:

```
A  A♯/B♭  B  C  C♯/D♭  D  D♯/E♭  E  F  F♯/G♭  G  G♯/A♭  (A again)
```

- That's **12 notes** in total, then it repeats.
- **No sharp/flat between B–C and E–F.** (On a piano: no black key there.)
- A♯ and B♭ are the **same pitch** with two names.
- The distance from one note to the next is a **half step** (semitone). **On guitar, one fret = one half step.**
- **Octave** = same letter, 12 half steps (12 frets) higher. Fret 12 on any string is an octave above the open string. That's why there's a double dot there.

### Why this matters to you
- Your tuner shows these note names.
- The **capo** moves everything up by half steps (see section 7).
- Chord names start with a note name: **A**m, **E**m, **G**, **D**m.

## 2. Chords: what makes them "major" or "minor"

A **chord** is 3 or more notes played together. The basic chord (a **triad**) has 3 notes: the **root** (which names the chord), the **third**, and the **fifth**.

| Chord | Notes | Sound | Notation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A minor** | A – C – E | Sad, dark, serious | Am, A min, A- |
| **E minor** | E – G – B | Sad, deep | Em |
| **G major** | G – B – D | Happy, bright, open | G (no "m" = major) |
| **D minor** | D – F – A | Sad, tender, dramatic | Dm |

- **"m" = minor.** No letter after the chord name = **major**.
- On the guitar, the 6 strings play only these 3 notes repeated in different octaves. Your Am chord `x02210` plays: A, E, A, C, E. Just A, C and E, doubled.
- **Em7** adds a 4th note (D) to Em: E–G–B–D. The "7" makes chords sound softer and jazzier. The record uses this color.

**Try it:** play Am, then play G. Do you hear the "sad → hopeful" difference? That emotional contrast is exactly what the song uses.

## 3. Keys: the "home base" of a song

A song's **key** is its home note and chord, where the music feels "resolved" or finished.

- **"Ain't No Sunshine" is in A minor.** Am is home. Every verse line starts and ends on Am.
- The other chords (Em, G, Dm) create **movement away from home**, and then the song returns to Am.
- **Listen for it:** play the progression and stop on Em or G. It feels unfinished, like a question. End on Am and it feels like an answer.

### The A minor chord family
In the key of A minor, the natural chords built from the notes A B C D E F G are:

| Degree | i | ii° | III | iv | v | VI | VII |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chord | **Am** | B dim | C | **Dm** | **Em** | F | **G** |

"Ain't No Sunshine" uses **i (Am), iv (Dm), v (Em) and VII (G)**, all from the same family. That's why they sound right together. Later, any song in A minor will draw from this same list.

Uppercase Roman numerals are major chords; lowercase are minor. Musicians say things like "the song is i – v – VII – i" and can then play it in any key.

## 4. Rhythm: beats, bars and counting

### Beat (pulse)
The steady pulse you'd tap your foot or nod your head to. Every song has one.

### Tempo
How fast the beat goes, in **BPM** (beats per minute). "Ain't No Sunshine" ≈ **76–80 BPM** (slow).

### Bars (measures) and time signatures
Beats are grouped into **bars** (also called measures). "Ain't No Sunshine" is in **4/4 time**:
- **4 beats per bar**, counted **"1, 2, 3, 4, 1, 2, 3, 4…"**
- **Beat 1** (the "downbeat") is the strongest. It's where chords usually change and where you "feel" the start.

In chord charts, a bar is shown with vertical lines: `| Am . . . | Em . G . |`
- Each `.` or chord name = one beat.
- `| Am . . . |` = Am for 4 beats
- `| Em . G . |` = Em for 2 beats, then G for 2 beats

### Subdividing: eighth notes
Each beat splits in two: **"1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &."** The "&" is called the **off-beat** or "upbeat." Downstrums go on the numbers, upstrums on the "&"s.

### Swing / shuffle feel
In "straight" time, "1 & 2 &" is evenly spaced: **da-da da-da**. In **swing** (common in soul and blues), the "&" is delayed: **daaa-da daaa-da**, like a lazy heartbeat or the rhythm of "hump-ty dump-ty." "Ain't No Sunshine" has a relaxed, lightly swung feel. Don't worry about it until Week 4; straight is fine to start.

### Pickup notes (anacrusis)
Sometimes the singer starts **before beat 1**. Those early notes are called **pickup notes**. Several lines in this song begin with a pickup: the first words come in just before the Am chord lands on "1." Feeling this is a Week 3 skill (see [04 — Singing While Playing](04-Singing-While-Playing.md)).

## 5. Melody vs. harmony vs. rhythm

| Part | What it is | Who does it this month |
|---|---|---|
| **Melody** | The tune: the single line of notes you'd hum | Your **voice** |
| **Harmony** | The chords underneath | Your **guitar** (left hand) |
| **Rhythm** | The pattern in time | Your **guitar** (right hand) + where you place words |

**The coordination challenge:** your voice's rhythm (where the syllables fall) is often *different* from your strumming rhythm. That's what makes singing while playing hard, and what [04 — Singing While Playing](04-Singing-While-Playing.md) solves.

## 6. Song form: sections

Songs are built from repeated sections:

| Section | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Intro** | The start before the singing |
| **Verse** | Sections with the same music but different words, usually telling the story |
| **Chorus** | The repeated "hook," same words each time. (In "Ain't No Sunshine" the title line works like a built-in hook that opens the verses. There's no separate chorus) |
| **Bridge** | A contrasting section, different from the verses. Here, the "I know" section |
| **Outro** | The ending |

Knowing the form means you always know **where you are** and **what's coming**, which is essential when you're doing two things at once.

## 7. The capo

A **capo** clamps across all the strings at a fret. It acts like a new nut, **raising every string (and every chord) by one half step per fret.**

- You keep using the **same chord shapes**, but they **sound higher**.
- Chord shapes are named relative to the capo. With the capo on fret 3, your "Am shape" sounds as **Cm**.
- **Why use it:** to move a song into **your** vocal range without learning new chords.

### Capo chart for this song (shapes: Am, Em, G, Dm)
| Capo fret | Key you're actually in | Am shape sounds as | Who it may suit |
|---|---|---|---|
| None | **A minor** (original) | Am | Most baritones, many men |
| 1 | B♭ minor | B♭m | |
| 2 | B minor | Bm | Higher male voices, low female voices |
| 3 | C minor | Cm | Altos, higher men |
| 4 | C♯ minor | C♯m | |
| 5 | D minor | Dm | Many female voices |
| 7 | E minor | Em | Higher female voices |

**How to use it:** clamp it **just behind** the fret (on the side toward the headstock), straight, firmly. Retune after putting it on (capos often pull strings slightly sharp).

**Alternatively**, higher voices can simply sing the original key **an octave up** with no capo. Try both; see the [Song Guide](05-Song-Guide-Aint-No-Sunshine.md#finding-your-key).

## 8. Scales (for your voice)

A **scale** is a set of notes in order.
- **Major scale:** do-re-mi-fa-so-la-ti-do. The "Sound of Music" scale. Happy.
- **Natural minor scale:** A-B-C-D-E-F-G-A (all the white keys from A). Sad.
- **Minor pentatonic scale:** 5 notes: A-C-D-E-G in A minor. The basis of blues, soul and rock melodies. **Much of the melody of "Ain't No Sunshine" is drawn from these same notes.**

For voice warm-ups you'll sing the first 5 notes of major and minor scales (Week 2). Singing the A minor pentatonic (A C D E G A) in Week 3 prepares your ear for the song.
