# 02 — Voice Fundamentals

This is the reference manual for the singing side of the course. Your voice is an instrument you can't see, and that you've been using (mostly for talking) your whole life. The job this month is to replace guesswork with a few reliable habits.

> **Everyone can learn to sing in tune.** True "tone deafness" (congenital amusia) affects only a small percentage of people. If you can hear the difference between a question and a statement in speech, you can almost certainly learn to match pitch. What most beginners call "tone deaf" is really **untrained**: the ear hears fine, but the voice hasn't learned to follow it yet. That's a coordination problem, and coordination problems get better with practice.

## 1. How your voice works (the 2-minute version)

Your voice has three parts, like any instrument:

| Part | Body part | Guitar equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| **Power source** | Lungs + breathing muscles (diaphragm, ribs, abs) | Your strumming hand |
| **Vibrator** | Vocal folds (vocal cords) in your larynx ("voice box," behind the Adam's apple) | The strings |
| **Resonator** | Throat, mouth, nose, sinuses (the "vocal tract") | The guitar body |

- Air from your lungs passes through your **vocal folds**, two small bands of tissue about 1–2 cm long. They vibrate (hundreds of times per second) and chop the airflow into sound.
- **Pitch** comes from how fast they vibrate. Stretched thinner and tighter = faster = higher pitch.
- **Volume** comes mostly from air pressure and how firmly the folds close.
- **Tone/color** comes from the shape of your throat and mouth: the resonator.
- **Words** come from your tongue, lips, teeth and jaw shaping the sound (articulation).

**What this means in practice:** most singing problems are really **breath** problems (not enough steady air) or **tension** problems (squeezing in the throat, jaw or tongue). Relaxed body + steady air = most of good singing.

## 2. Posture for singing

### Standing (the ideal for pure vocal practice)
- Feet hip-width apart, weight balanced, knees soft (not locked).
- Spine long. Imagine a string lifting the top (crown) of your head.
- Chest comfortably lifted (not puffed out like a soldier).
- Shoulders down and relaxed, arms loose at your sides.
- Chin level, **not jutting forward and not tilted up** to reach high notes (the #1 posture mistake singers make).
- Jaw loose; tongue resting forward with its tip behind your bottom teeth.

### Sitting with the guitar (most of this month)
- Same upper body: tall spine, relaxed shoulders, level chin.
- Sit on the **front half of the chair**. Leaning back collapses your breathing.
- **Don't hunch over the guitar to look at your fingers.** A dropped head squashes the throat. Glance with your eyes, not your whole neck.
- Guitar body shouldn't press into your belly so hard that you can't expand when you breathe.

### Quick posture reset (do it before every singing exercise)
1. Raise your arms over your head. Take a breath.
2. Lower your arms slowly and **keep your chest where it was** when your arms were up.
3. Roll your shoulders back and down 3 times.
4. Nod "yes" gently and shake "no" gently, to free up the neck.

## 3. Breathing

### Belly breathing (diaphragmatic breathing)
Most people breathe high and shallow into the chest when nervous, and nervousness is exactly how singing feels at first. Singers breathe **low**.

**Learn it (Day 1):**
1. Lie on your back on the floor, knees bent. Put a book on your belly.
2. Breathe in through your nose. The **book rises**. Your chest barely moves.
3. Breathe out slowly. The book lowers.
4. Now sit or stand and do the same with one hand on your belly and one on your chest. **Belly hand moves; chest hand stays still.** Feel your lower ribs expand out to the sides (and even your back).
5. **Shoulders never rise** when you inhale. Watch in a mirror.

### Why it matters
- Low breaths hold more air.
- You get finer control over how quickly the air comes out (which gives you steadier notes).
- It keeps tension away from your throat.

### Breath support ("appoggio," or just "support")
Support means **controlling the exhale** so air comes out steady and slowly, rather than all at once. Think of it like letting air out of a balloon by pinching the neck: steady and even.

When you sing, **keep your ribs gently expanded** for as long as you can while the belly slowly moves in. Don't collapse your chest at the start of a phrase.

### Breathing exercises (all in [06 — Daily Routines](06-Daily-Routines.md))
| Exercise | How | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| **4-4-8** | Inhale 4 counts, hold 4 (gently, throat open, not locked), exhale 8 on "ssss" | Control |
| **Hiss ladder** | Inhale 4, hiss "sssss" as long and evenly as possible. Time it | Week 1: 15 s → Week 4: 30+ s |
| **Staccato "ha" / "ts"** | Short, sharp puffs: "ts-ts-ts-ts." Feel your belly bounce inward on each | Engages the support muscles |
| **Sip breath** | Inhale quickly through a small "O" mouth, like sipping through a straw, in 1 count | Fast low breaths between song lines |
| **Surprise breath** | Silent, quick, low breath through the mouth, like a pleasant surprise | The breath you'll actually use while singing |

### Planning breaths in a song
Singers decide **where** they'll breathe in advance, just like they decide chords. In "Ain't No Sunshine" the melody has natural gaps after each line, and the [Song Guide](05-Song-Guide-Aint-No-Sunshine.md) marks them (**✓** = breath). The famous "I know" section is the one place you need to plan carefully.

**Silent breaths.** A loud, gasping inhale ("hhhuuh") means your throat is tight. Aim for silent, open-throat inhales, as if you're about to yawn.

## 4. Warming up (and why)

Your vocal folds are muscle and tissue. Just like you'd stretch before running, a 5–10 minute warm-up:
- Increases blood flow to the folds
- Reduces the chance of strain
- Makes high and low notes easier
- Helps you find your "singing" coordination (as opposed to "talking")

### The core warm-ups: SOVT exercises
**SOVT = Semi-Occluded Vocal Tract.** That means partly closing off your mouth (with lips, a straw, or a hum). It creates back-pressure that helps your vocal folds vibrate efficiently with **less effort** and less risk. Speech therapists and voice teachers use these constantly. They're the safest, most effective warm-ups there are.

| Exercise | How | Tips |
|---|---|---|
| **Lip trills (lip bubbles)** | Blow air through loose, closed lips so they flap like a motorboat, "brrrrr," then add voice (sound) | Can't do it? Press gently on your cheeks with your index fingers at the corners of your mouth. Keeps air flowing steadily |
| **Straw phonation** | Hum through a drinking straw (a narrow coffee stirrer is even better). Slide up and down | The single best warm-up, according to many voice scientists. Also great for "resetting" a tired voice |
| **Humming** | Lips closed gently, teeth apart, "mmmm." Feel buzzing on your lips and nose | If you feel buzz in your throat instead of your face, you're pushing. Lighten up |
| **Tongue trills** | Rolled "rrrrr" (if you can do it) | Optional |
| **"Ng" (as in sing)** | Hold the "ng" sound at the end of "sing" and slide around | Relaxes the throat, places sound forward |

### Sirens and slides
Slide smoothly from your lowest comfortable note to your highest comfortable note and back, like a siren, on a lip trill, a straw, or "ng" / "oo." **No jumps, no pushing.** This connects your registers (see section 9).

### Five-note scales
Sing "1-2-3-4-5-4-3-2-1" (do-re-mi-fa-so-fa-mi-re-do) on "mah," "mee," "moh," or "noo," then move up by a half step and repeat. Use a piano app or a YouTube "5 note scale vocal warm up" track to follow. Details in [06 — Daily Routines](06-Daily-Routines.md).

### Cool-downs (1–2 minutes, after long sessions)
Gentle downward sirens on a lip trill or straw, then humming in your speaking range. They bring your voice back to "talking mode."

## 5. Pitch: hearing it and matching it

### What "in tune" means
When you sing "in tune," the note your voice makes vibrates at (almost exactly) the same frequency as the reference: the guitar, the piano, the recording. "Out of tune" means **sharp** (slightly too high) or **flat** (slightly too low). Beginners are most often **flat**, usually because of low energy or not enough breath.

### Pitch-matching method (Week 1 onward)
1. Pluck an open string on the guitar, starting with the **A string (string 5)**, or the **D string (4)** for higher voices. (A tuner app gives you a reference note too.)
2. **Listen** to it for 2 seconds. Don't sing yet. Hear it in your head ("audiate" it).
3. **Hum** it softly.
4. Listen: does your hum blend with the guitar (smooth, one sound) or clash (a "wah-wah" beating, or clearly two different sounds)?
5. If unsure, **slide**: start below and slowly slide up until it locks in. You'll feel and hear a click into place as the two sounds merge.
6. Check with a **tuner app**. Sing into it and see what note it shows. (Apps like "Vocal Pitch Monitor," "Sing Sharp," "Pitch Detector," or any chromatic tuner work.)
7. Once your hum matches, sing it on "ah."

**If your voice is much higher than the guitar string (common for women and kids):** sing the same note **an octave up**. It's the "same" note, just higher, and it counts. The tuner will show the same letter.

### Exercises for the ear
| Exercise | How | Week |
|---|---|---|
| **Match the string** | Pluck a string, hum it, check with a tuner | 1+ |
| **Chord tone hunt** | Strum Am. Can you hum any note that "fits"? Now find the **lowest** A in the chord | 2+ |
| **Up or down?** | Have an app or friend play two notes. Is the second higher or lower? | 1–2 |
| **Echo singing** | Play a 3-note phrase on one string, then sing it back | 2+ |
| **Sing then check** | Sing a note you *imagine* is A, then pluck the A string. How close were you? | 3+ |
| **Melody echo** | Play 2 seconds of the recording, pause, sing it back | 3+ |

### Why the guitar helps your pitch (and hurts it)
- **Helps:** chords give you a harmonic "bed" to sing on, which makes it easier to hear when you're off.
- **Hurts:** when you're concentrating on your fingers, your ear "switches off" and your pitch drifts. That's why the [Singing While Playing](04-Singing-While-Playing.md) method builds up slowly.

## 6. Finding your range and voice type

### Find your range (Day 4 exercise)
1. Open a tuner or pitch app.
2. Sing "ah" or "ee" at a comfortable speaking pitch. Note it. That's around the **center of your speaking range.**
3. Slide down, on "ah" or a lip trill, as low as you can **comfortably** (without croaking or losing the sound). Note the lowest clear note.
4. Slide up as high as you can **without straining** (it's OK if it goes light or airy/falsetto). Note the highest comfortable note.
5. Write your **comfortable range** in your log. **Don't** push to find your absolute limits. That's not useful and can strain.

### Rough voice-type guide (for orientation only)
| Voice type | Typical comfortable range | Usually |
|---|---|---|
| Bass | E2 – E4 | Lowest male voices |
| Baritone | A2 – A4 | Most common adult male voice |
| Tenor | C3 – C5 | Higher male voices |
| Alto / contralto | F3 – F5 | Lower female voices |
| Mezzo-soprano | A3 – A5 | Most common adult female voice |
| Soprano | C4 – C6 | Higher female voices |

> **Your "voice type" doesn't matter much this month.** Its only practical use: deciding whether to sing "Ain't No Sunshine" in the original key or move it with a capo. Bill Withers was a baritone, and the melody sits in a comfortable middle-low range for most male voices. See the [Song Guide: Finding Your Key](05-Song-Guide-Aint-No-Sunshine.md#finding-your-key).

## 7. Tone and resonance

**Resonance** is how the sound "rings" in your throat, mouth and face. The same note can sound thin, muffled, bright or warm depending on where it resonates.

- **Forward placement ("mask" resonance):** aim to feel buzzing on your lips, nose and cheekbones. Humming shows you this feeling. Sing "mmm-ah": keep the buzz of the "mmm" as you open to "ah."
- **Open throat:** the feeling at the start of a yawn: soft palate lifted, throat wide. **Just the start** of a yawn. A full yawn pushes the larynx down and makes you sound hooty.
- **Relaxed jaw:** the jaw should drop, loose. Test it: put your fingers on the hinges of your jaw (in front of your ears) and open. If the muscles bulge, you're clenching.
- **Relaxed tongue:** the tip rests behind your lower front teeth. A tongue pulled back into the throat makes the sound muffled and swallowed.

## 8. Vowels, consonants and diction

- **Sing on the vowels.** Vowels carry the tone; consonants just start and stop them. On a long note, get to the vowel quickly and **stay on it.**
- A common mistake: closing to the consonant too early. On a word like "gone," hold "gaw—" and put the "n" at the very end.
- **Diphthongs** (double vowels like "i" in "time" = "ah-ee"): hold the **first** vowel, and add the second only at the very end. "Taaaaah-eem," not "tah-eeeeeem."
- **American soul and folk style is conversational.** You don't need to over-enunciate like in choir. Sing words roughly as you'd say them, just stretched and on pitch.
- **Final consonants**: make them clear but light, like "tapping" them, not spitting them.

## 9. Registers: chest voice, head voice and the "break"

| Register | Feels like | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| **Chest voice** | Vibration in your chest, like your speaking voice | Full, strong, "talking" sound |
| **Head voice** | Vibration in your head/face, lighter | Lighter, sweeter, more "floaty" |
| **Falsetto** | Very light, often breathy, "whoo" or cartoon-owl sound | Airy, thin. Common in soul music |
| **Mix** | A blend: some of each | What trained pop singers use for high notes |

- The **break (passaggio)** is the spot where your voice "flips" or cracks going from chest to head voice. Everyone has one.
- **For this song you'll mostly be in chest voice**, which is easy news. The melody lives in a relaxed, speaking-ish register. You probably won't hit the break if you're in the right key.
- Sirens and lip trills (daily) gradually smooth the break. That's useful long term, even if this song doesn't need it much.
- **Never force chest voice higher by shouting.** If a note feels strained, lighten the sound or change the key.

## 10. Vocal health: rules you don't break

### Daily habits
- **Hydrate.** Drink water through the day. The vocal folds are hydrated from the inside (what you drink hours before) and by the humidity of the air you breathe. Rough target: pale yellow urine.
- **Steam or humid air** helps. Breathe steam from a bowl or a hot shower for 5 minutes, or use a humidifier in dry months.
- **Warm up before you sing, every time.** Even 3 minutes of lip trills.
- **Sleep.** A tired body means a tired voice.

### Things that hurt your voice
| Avoid | Why |
|---|---|
| **Shouting and screaming** (sports games, loud bars) | The #1 cause of vocal injury |
| **Talking over loud noise** | You push without noticing |
| **Whispering** a lot | Surprisingly, it's more straining than soft talking |
| **Excessive throat clearing** | It slams the folds together. **Swallow or sip water instead.** |
| **Singing through pain or hoarseness** | It can cause nodules (callus-like bumps on the folds) |
| **Smoking and vaping** | Dry out and irritate the folds |
| **Lots of alcohol and caffeine right before singing** | Drying |
| **Acid reflux** (late heavy meals, spicy/fatty food before bed) | Stomach acid irritates the larynx. Eat 2–3 hours before lying down |
| **Dairy right before singing** | For some people it thickens mucus. Test it on yourself |
| **Menthol lozenges and medicated sprays "to sing through it"** | They mask symptoms; they don't fix anything |

### Signs to stop and rest
- Hoarseness, scratchiness, or pain during or after singing
- Your voice "cutting out" or losing the top of your range
- A feeling of a lump or effort in your throat
- **Next-morning hoarseness** means you overdid it. Take a vocal rest day (talk less, no singing), hydrate, and drop intensity next time.

### See a doctor (ideally an ENT/laryngologist, or a speech-language pathologist who works with voice) if
- Hoarseness lasts **more than 2 weeks**
- Singing or speaking is painful
- You cough up blood
- You suddenly lose part of your range and it doesn't come back

### How long to sing each day (Month 1)
| Week | Active singing time | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10–15 min | Mostly warm-ups and pitch work |
| 2 | 15–20 min | |
| 3 | 20–25 min | Break it up with guitar-only sections |
| 4 | 25–35 min | Short rests between full run-throughs |

Your voice is like any other muscle: **build duration gradually.** Silent practice (guitar-only, or mentally singing the melody) counts as practice and costs your voice nothing.

## 11. Confidence and hearing your own voice

- **You'll probably dislike your recorded voice.** Inside your head you hear yourself partly through bone, which adds bass. Recordings sound thinner and "not like you." After a couple of weeks of recording daily, this fades.
- **Volume matters for pitch.** Singing timidly (too quietly) often makes you go flat and breathy. Aim for a comfortable, confident "speaking across a room" level. You're not performing for the neighbors, just using a supported voice.
- **Mistakes are data.** When a note is off, don't cringe. Note it and replay it slowly.
- **Sing along with the record a lot.** Tons of it. In the car, in the shower. It's low-pressure and extremely effective.
- **Put a hand over one ear** (or cup it, like a studio singer with headphones pushed off one side). You'll hear yourself much more clearly, and your pitch will improve instantly. Use this trick whenever you're checking pitch.

## 12. Singing in the style of this song

"Ain't No Sunshine" is a **soul/folk** song with a restrained, intimate delivery. That's perfect for a beginner, because nothing about it requires power, belting, or vocal gymnastics.

- **Conversational.** Sing like you're telling someone something sad, not like you're on stage.
- **Warm, relaxed chest voice.** Nothing forced.
- **Space.** The phrases are short, with rests in between. Don't rush to fill the gaps. The silence is part of the song.
- **The "I know" section** is rhythmic and chant-like: short, repeated, percussive. Think of it as rhythm, not melody, sitting on one pitch area. Breathing (and counting) is the whole challenge there. See the [Song Guide](05-Song-Guide-Aint-No-Sunshine.md).
- **Small emotional build:** a bit more intensity in the later verses, pulling back at the end.
- **Don't imitate Bill Withers' exact voice.** Sing it in your own voice, in your own key. That's what makes a cover worth listening to.
